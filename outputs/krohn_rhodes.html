Wheels & Funnels

Krohn–Rhodes for two-button machines, by hand.

Below is a drawing where every arrow starts and ends on another arrow. (No dots, no vertices: arrows all the way down. These are called pergraphs.) Each arrow has exactly one arrow it starts on (its source) and one it points into (its sink).

Put a token on an arrow. Press s, and the token slides back to the arrow's source. Press t, and it slides forward to the arrow's sink. That's it. That's the whole machine.

1. Two buttons

Press buttons for a bit. The token always ends up somewhere, and where it ends up depends only on where it was and which button you pressed. So this drawing is a machine: a finite automaton with two buttons. Every such machine can be drawn this way, and every drawing like this is such a machine.

2. Ink everywhere

Now put a token on every arrow, and press buttons. Watch what happens when two tokens land on the same arrow.

They merge, and nothing can ever split them again: from now on, both tokens get exactly the same instructions. Merging is forgetting. Once two tokens are merged, the machine has lost the information of where they came from.

Puzzle: can you merge all tokens into one? A word that does that is called a synchronizing word. It's a reset button, built out of other buttons.

Here's a harder one. This machine is known as Černý's machine:

Černý's machine with n arrows needs (n−1)² presses to synchronize, here 9. Černý conjectured in 1964 that no machine with n states ever needs more than that, if it can be synchronized at all. Sixty years later that's still open: the best known upper bound is about n³/6. You're allowed to be the one who proves it.

4. Wheels

Some machines never merge anything. Here each button just moves the tokens around. Colour them, and see what shuffles you can produce:

Only rotations. However hard you try, you can't swap just two colours. Now a machine where you can:

A machine that only shuffles is a wheel. The set of shuffles you can reach is closed under doing one after the other, and every shuffle can be undone by more pressing: it's a group. The first wheel's group has 3 shuffles (the rotations, called ℤ₃), and the second's has all 6 (called S₃).

5. Funnels

At the other extreme: a machine that does nothing but merge.

s sends everything to one arrow and t sends everything to the other. The machine forgets where it was, and remembers exactly one thing: which button you pressed last. That's one bit of memory, called a flip-flop, and it's the simplest possible funnel.

6. The odometer

How do you build a big machine out of small ones? Here is a four-arrow wheel. Name the arrows 00, 01, 10 and 11 (in binary), and watch the two bits:

Pressing s counts up. The right bit just flips, every time, paying no attention to the left bit. The left bit flips only when the right bit rolls over from 1 to 0: a carry. And t? It clears the left bit, but only when the right bit is 0.

So the four-arrow machine is really two one-bit machines chained together. The right bit runs on its own, and the left bit's behaviour depends on the right bit, but never the other way around. That one-way chaining is called a cascade, and it's the only glue we'll need.

7. The theorem

Krohn–Rhodes (1965): every finite machine can be emulated by a cascade, where each part is either a funnel (flip-flops) or a wheel that can't be split any further.

8. Prime machines

The wheels that can't be split any further are the simple groups. The theorem says they, plus the flip-flop, are the prime machines: everything is built out of them. Each of them can itself be drawn as a two-button arrow machine:

ℤ₂, ℤ₃, ℤ₅, … are the easy primes: rings. The first genuinely strange one is A₅, the 60 even shuffles of 5 things (the symmetries of an icosahedron). You need at least 5 arrows for it, because every wheel on 4 or fewer things splits into rings. Every finite simple group can be generated by two elements (a theorem that leans on the classification of finite simple groups), so every prime machine has a two-button drawing like these.

The random 7-arrow machine in chapter 7 has an S₅ level, so an A₅ inside. That seems to be typical, not lucky. Two random shuffles almost always generate all shuffles (Dixon's theorem), so random machines tend to hide big wheels.
